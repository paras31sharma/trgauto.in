EV vs Petrol: The Real Breakeven

Most EV comparisons stop at fuel savings. That tells only one part of the story. The upfront premium, opportunity cost, resale value and where you charge can change the answer.

₹2.69L example premium10,000 km/year5-year viewPublished September 2026
The core idea An EV can cost much less to run per kilometre and still take years to financially recover its higher purchase price. The right comparison is total cost of ownership, not just fuel versus electricity.

The Nexon example

Take a petrol Nexon and a comparable Nexon EV. For this worked example, we use the assumptions from the TRG reel: a ₹2.69 lakh purchase-price difference, 10,000 km of driving per year, petrol at ₹102/litre and public DC charging at ₹22/kWh.

At those inputs, five years of operating savings are about ₹2.41 lakh. That is meaningful — but it is still below the ₹2.69 lakh upfront premium.

5-year fuel/energy saving ≈ ₹48,292 × 5 = ₹2.41 lakh

Then comes the hidden cost: opportunity cost

Suppose the extra ₹2.69 lakh required for the EV was instead left in a 7% fixed deposit. At annual compounding, it grows to roughly ₹3.77 lakh after five years — about ₹1.08 lakh of foregone interest is tied to choosing the more expensive car.

₹2.69L × (1.07)5 ≈ ₹3.77L

A simple way to communicate the idea is to compare the five-year opportunity-cost-adjusted amount with the annual running-cost saving: ₹3.77L ÷ ₹48,292 ≈ 7.8 years.

Important: this 7.8-year figure is a rough communication shortcut, not a full discounted-cash-flow calculation. A rigorous model would discount yearly cash flows and include resale value, charging mix and other ownership costs.

Resale can change the result again

Resale value is often ignored in EV-versus-petrol break-even posts. That matters because you are not paying only to use the car — you are also buying an asset that you may later sell.

Current Indian used-car research points to an important caveat: the 2026 Autocar India–Spinny Mobility Intelligence Report, based on more than 11,000 vehicle transactions across nine cities, says EV resale still lacks predictable benchmarks because of issues such as battery-health assessment and rapid technology changes. The same study puts average depreciation across new cars at 21% after one year, 33% after three years and 41% after five years, while noting that retention varies significantly by model.

That does not mean every EV will resell worse than every petrol car. It means resale should be model-specific rather than treated as a fixed percentage for all EVs.

Home charging vs public charging

The ₹22/kWh public-DC assumption in this example is deliberately important. EV economics can look very different when a large share of charging happens at home, or when charging is mostly public.

Mostly home charging Lower energy cost can shorten the operating-cost payback.
Mostly public charging Higher charging tariffs can reduce the annual saving.
Mixed charging Use a weighted average based on your actual routine.
No home charger Time, convenience and charger availability become practical costs too.

What a proper EV-vs-petrol comparison should include

01. Purchase premium Compare equivalent variants, not just the cheapest EV with a more expensive petrol trim.
02. Energy cost Use your real annual kilometres and realistic fuel/charging prices.
03. Opportunity cost Account for what the extra upfront money could have earned elsewhere.
04. Resale value Estimate the likely value at your ownership horizon using model-specific evidence.
05. Charging mix Separate home, workplace and public charging.
06. Other ownership costs Add insurance, service, tyres, financing and taxes/fees where they differ materially.

The takeaway

EVs can have a compelling running-cost advantage. But running-cost advantage is not the same thing as financial break-even.

For a buyer doing 10,000 km a year, the difference between a good EV decision and a poor financial assumption can come down to just a few inputs: how much extra you pay up front, where you charge, what your money could earn, and what the car is worth when you sell it.

TRG framework Compare price difference + energy savings + opportunity cost + resale value + charging mix. Then decide whether the EV works for your actual usage pattern.
